Pad Thai

Thailand's wok-fried rice noodles in a three-part tamarind, palm sugar and fish sauce glaze, cooked two portions at a time so the noodles fry rather than steam.

  • Cuisine: Thai
  • Category: Main
  • Yield: 2 servings, one batch
  • Prep: 25 minutes
  • Cook: 10 minutes
  • Total: 35 minutes

Ingredients

  • 180 g dried flat rice noodles, 5 mm wide
  • Sauce: 60 g tamarind concentrate
  • Sauce: 60 g palm sugar, chopped
  • Sauce: 45 ml fish sauce
  • Sauce: 1 tbsp water
  • 8 raw prawns, peeled and deveined
  • 80 g firm tofu, in small cubes
  • 2 tbsp dried shrimp, roughly chopped
  • 2 tbsp sweet preserved radish, chopped
  • 2 eggs
  • 3 garlic cloves, finely chopped
  • 3 tbsp neutral oil or lard
  • 100 g bean sprouts
  • 40 g garlic chives, in 4 cm lengths
  • 40 g roasted peanuts, coarsely crushed
  • Lime wedges, to serve
  • Chilli flakes and sugar, to serve

Instructions

  1. Soak the noodles in room-temperature water for 45–60 minutes, until pliable but still firm and opaque at the core. Drain well. Never boil them.
  2. Melt the palm sugar with the tamarind, fish sauce and water over a low heat until syrupy, then cool. It should taste aggressively sour, sweet and salty all at once, because the noodles will dilute it.
  3. Set every ingredient within arm's reach of the hob. The cook takes four minutes and there is no time to chop mid-wok.
  4. Heat the wok until smoking, add the oil, then the tofu, dried shrimp and preserved radish. Fry 1 minute. Add the garlic for 15 seconds, then the prawns until just pink. Push everything up the side of the wok.
  5. Add the drained noodles and the sauce. Toss constantly for 2–3 minutes. If they resist softening, add water a tablespoon at a time, never more.
  6. Push the noodles aside, crack the eggs into the clear space and scramble them lightly until just set, then fold them through.
  7. Add the bean sprouts, garlic chives and half the peanuts. Toss 30 seconds so they heat through but stay crunchy.
  8. Plate immediately with the remaining peanuts, a lime wedge, chilli flakes and sugar, so each person balances their own.

Cook two portions, never four

A domestic hob cannot keep a wok hot enough for a double batch. Overload it and the temperature crashes, the noodles release starch, and you get a sticky clump instead of separate glazed strands.

Cook in sequence and hold the first batch. Two minutes of waiting beats one bad plate.

Tamarind, not ketchup

What makes it taste Thai

The sour backbone is tamarind. Tomato ketchup, which appears in a lot of Western recipes, makes the dish sweet and red and quite different.

Concentrate strengths vary wildly. Taste the sauce before it hits the wok and adjust with more tamarind or sugar. Aim for something that makes you wince slightly on its own.
